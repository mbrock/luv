#+title: Temporal reconstruction in the Luft atelier
#+startup: overview

Luft's temporal antialiasing is deliberately a renderer experiment rather
than a new engine-wide temporal framework.  Both backends draw a jittered,
single-sample scene at =*render-scale*= and write current-to-previous motion
beside it before the lens and presentation pass.  Vulkan reconstructs the
output with Luft's inspectable shader, a neighbourhood pass and a resolve over
two alternating history images; Metal gives colour, depth, and motion to a
MetalFX temporal scaler that owns its opaque history, unless
=*temporal-reconstruction*= asks for the shader there too.  Luvcraft has its
own resolve of the same shape, described in #HDC5D8.

The work also answers two architectural questions the small greenfield
renderer made cheap to ask.  The renderer wanted explicit owners for a frozen
frame and for every extent-sized surface, plus revisions on published scene
products.  The mathematical shader language already had all the expressions
the Vulkan resolve needed.  The portable GPU layer grew only the concrete
vocabulary both consumers earned: ordered colour attachments, a two-half-float
motion format, and a temporal-scaler command whose Metal implementation exposes
the native texture contract and ordering fence.

* TAA reconstructs samples; it is not a blur
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: DOEDL3
:END:

One rasterized frame samples each pixel once.  Luft moves that sample to a
different subpixel on each frame with a sixteen-sample Halton(2,3) sequence,
then reprojects the last resolved picture so samples from different frames
describe the same world points.  A static camera therefore gathers sixteen
positions within a pixel instead of repeatedly gathering its centre.  That is
why a temporal scene rasterizes one sample per pixel: 4× MSAA on top of it
cost geometry bandwidth for edges the jitter already resolves, and made
depth and motion need resolves with no honest meaning at a silhouette.  MSAA
remains only when temporal reconstruction is off (#O18BY6).  A moving
camera can keep the old evidence only where motion says where it went and the
new neighbourhood says it is still plausible.

This ordering matters.  The scene is jittered before rasterization; temporal
resolve happens in linear floating-point colour; the photographic lens and the
conversion to the final sRGB attachment happen afterward.  Applying a screen
blur first would only soften one aliased sample.  Accumulating after the lens
would feed its chromatic dispersion and focus blur back into history.

* One frozen view defines one frame
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: VATCML
:END:

The fly camera is a mutable interactive object.  Reading it independently in
the surface, sky, and resolve setup would let one encoded frame contain several
moments.  [[lisp:luft.render::capture-frame-view]] instead copies position,
basis, projection, and jitter into a =frame-view= once at
the frame boundary.  That immutable value is /current/; the value published by
the last completed encoding is /previous/.

[[lisp:luft.render::temporal-jitter]] returns clip-space offsets, so one pixel
of displacement is =2/width= by =2/height=.  Every terrain vertex adds the
current offset times homogeneous W to its clip position.  The fullscreen sky
does not move its triangle; it subtracts the same offset while reconstructing
the view ray, which makes the sky and geometry samples belong to one jittered
camera.

The shared frame block carries the previous view, current and previous jitter,
and inverse internal extent.  Vulkan's resolve reuses the otherwise-padding W
components of two previous-view lanes for history validity and blend weight,
so restoring the shader path did not change the established 27-vec4 ABI.

* The frame graph resolves before post-processing
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: 4I4Y3Z
:END:

#+begin_src mermaid
flowchart TB
  S["jittered surface + sky, one sample"] --> C["RGBA16F current colour"]
  S --> M["RG16F current-to-previous motion"]
  S --> D["D32 visibility"]
  C --> N["neighbourhood, scene size"]
  M --> N
  D --> N
  N --> L["YCoCg bounds + nearest motion"]
  L --> R["temporal resolve, output size"]
  C --> R
  A["history: last frame's output image"] --> R
  R --> B["resolved RGBA16F: the other image"]
  B -. "roles swap next frame" .-> A
  B --> P["lens or plain present"]
  P --> O["output attachment"]
#+end_src

| Product | Format | Meaning |
|-
| Current colour | =RGBA16F= | This frame's jittered, pre-presentation colour; alpha is current focus metadata |
| Motion | =RG16F= | Unjittered current-to-previous displacement in UV units |
| Depth | =D32= | Surface visibility; consumed by MetalFX and by the shader neighbourhood's velocity dilation |
| Bounds | 2 × =RGBA16F= | Per scene texel: YCoCg minimum and maximum, nearest motion in their W lanes |
| Resolved colour | =RGBA16F= | The shader alternates two output images, reading one and writing the other; MetalFX owns native history behind one output |
| Output | Renderer format | Lens-processed or plainly presented result, normally sRGB |

[[lisp:luft.render::encode-renderer-frame]] keeps this semantic order on both
backends.  Vulkan ends the surface pass, gathers each scene texel's
neighbourhood once, then resolves current colour, those bounds, and last
frame's output into the other output image before compositing flames and
presenting.  The two images swap roles each frame, so nothing is copied.  Metal ends the
surface pass by signaling the scaler's fence, encodes MetalFX on the Metal 4
command encoder, then makes the composite pass wait before sampling resolved
colour.  A cold or invalid frame rejects history; only after resolve encoding
does Luft publish the frozen view.

* Motion points from the current sample to its old address
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: C7WIN4
:END:

Let $q_t$ be the unjittered UV position of a world point under the current
view, $q_{t-1}$ its position under the previous view, and $j_t$ the current
clip-space jitter.  The motion attachment stores

#+begin_example
v = q(t-1) - q(t)
#+end_example

without jitter.  The current raster sample is displaced by $j_t/2$, but
resolved history lives on the fixed output grid.  Resolve therefore addresses
history at

#+begin_example
u + v
#+end_example

Keeping jitter out of this address lets different raster samples accumulate
into one stable output pixel.  The motion shader owns reprojection and the
resolve shader owns the sampling pattern.  Static terrain writes motion by
projecting its interpolated world point through both frozen views; the sky
projects the same world direction from each camera origin.  Motion is loaded with exact
=texel-load= rather than filtered at silhouettes, where blending foreground
and background velocities would invent a third surface.

Ordinary and temporal surface fragments are paired from one source body.
The ordinary form advertises only colour location zero; the temporal form adds
motion at location one.  Every Luft material -- flat, rounded, chamfered,
field, ink, paper, stock, and sky -- therefore follows the same motion
convention on both Vulkan and Metal.

* Resolve admits old colour through a shaped neighbourhood
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: C4ED2V
:END:

[[lisp:luft.render.shaders:temporal-neighbourhood-fragment-specification]]
gathers the current 3×3 neighbourhood once per scene texel, converts it to
YCoCg, and stores the componentwise minimum and maximum.  The resolve draws
several output pixels per scene texel when upscaling, so gathering there would
repeat the same eighteen taps for each of them; Moppe measured that split at
3.4 ms becoming 1.3 at 7680×2160.  YCoCg makes this a box in luminance and two
chroma axes rather than three unrelated RGB axes, so clipping changes hue less
readily.

The same pass keeps the motion of the /nearest/ of the nine texels.  At a
silhouette the centre may be background while its neighbour is the moving
foreground, and history must follow the foreground or leave its old colour on
the newly revealed background.

[[lisp:luft.render.shaders:temporal-resolve-fragment-specification]] samples
the current scene at the unjittered address, reads the texel's bounds, and
fetches reprojected history with a five-tap Catmull-Rom filter.  Bilinear
history blurs a little more every frame it is reprojected; the bicubic
fetch keeps the accumulated image sharp.

The configured history share is 0.9.  It falls as screen motion rises;
an out-of-frame address or invalid history makes it zero.  The YCoCg box clips
old colour but deliberately does not reject alternate luminance at a static
silhouette: that coverage is the evidence TAA exists to retain.  Alpha is never
accumulated: Luft's lens reads alpha as this frame's focus metadata, so resolve
copies it from the current centre.

* History is a semantic cache
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: OWG6ZD
:END:

A texture from the preceding submission is not necessarily history of the
same picture.  The current viewer invalidates it when any of these occur:

- a new extent-sized target generation is published;
- a real mesh/material/lighting publication replaces renderer residency;
- a viewer command changes a discrete rendered semantic such as construction
  lines or bevel width; or
- [[lisp:luft.render::frame-views-continuous-p]] judges translation, rotation,
  or focal change to be a camera cut rather than motion.

Each boundary produces one current-only frame before accumulation resumes.
No-op residency requests preserve the exact target cohort and its history.

* Frame resources are one extent-sized ownership cohort
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: T7RQTI
:END:

TAA made a loose collection of renderer texture slots too easy to publish
halfway through a resize.  A
[[lisp:luft.render::renderer-target-resources]] now owns depth, current colour,
optional motion, resolve output, Vulkan history or a MetalFX scaler, composite
colour, their views, and the bind groups that depend on them.
[[lisp:luft.render::make-renderer-target-generation]] constructs a complete
candidate; resize publishes it as one value, resets history, and then retires
the old cohort.  Destruction walks the opposite dependency order: bind groups,
scaler, views, textures, then the longer-lived layouts and device resources.

Scene storage growth follows the same rule.  Sites, cell bits, cell stock
slots, and their bind group form one generation.  If any buffer is too small,
[[lisp:luft.render::upload-scene]] builds and fills replacements for all three,
creates the candidate bind group, publishes them together, and only then
retires the old generation.  Buffers that already fit are updated in place;
capacities double on growth so editing does not rebuild the cohort for every
new face.

* The shader language was ready; both HALs needed MRT
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: CZGBC9
:END:

No new mathematical shader operation was required.  The language already
represented several location outputs, texture sampling, exact =texel-load=,
uniform and storage resources, vector =min=/=max=/=clamp=, derivatives, and
source abstractions.  A small Luft-local macro makes ordinary/temporal shader
pairs; putting that policy into the generic graph would have confused a
renderer variant with a new mathematical idea.

The GPU layer did need to tell the truth about a fragment stage with more than
one result.  =:rg16-float= now names two half-float motion lanes, including its
four-byte texel size and native Vulkan and Metal formats.  Vulkan render
pipelines, compatible-render-pass caching, attachment descriptions and
references, blend states, framebuffers, clear values, and pass validation all
consume an ordered target list.  Metal pipeline compilation and render-pass
attachment setup now do the same.  The generic descriptors still describe a
one-target pass without a special case at their callers.

This is the useful boundary: multiple render targets, exact same-format image
copies, and a format are portable GPU vocabulary; temporal rejection, history,
and camera-cut policy belong to the renderer.

* Two backend resolves share one semantic boundary
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: D7GZA6
:END:

The frozen current/previous views, jitter sequence, current colour, depth,
motion meaning, invalidation decision, and extent cohort are backend-neutral
semantic inputs.  [[lisp:luft.render::temporal-resolve-kind]] selects an
implementation at the device boundary: Vulkan compiles Luft's shader resolve;
Metal creates a fixed-extent [[lisp:gpu-temporal-scaler]] and does not compile
that shader at all.

The scaler is a narrow capability, not a portable imitation of MetalFX.  Its
descriptor fixes input and output extents and the four formats.  Creation asks
MetalFX for the exact usage of each texture before Luft constructs the extent
cohort.  Each frame supplies colour, conventional depth, normalized
current-to-previous motion, pixel-space jitter, and the semantic reset bit.
Metal owns the neutral exposure texture and the fence required to order its
render, scaler, and post stages; destruction retires the scaler before the
textures and views on which it depends.

This leaves renderer policy in Luft and native reconstruction in the backend.
The default full effect list is =(:sky :lens :taa)= on both platforms, but
neither backend has to pretend that the other's history representation is its
own.

* Luvcraft resolves from depth, not from motion vectors
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: HDC5D8
:END:

Luvcraft has a dozen scene pipelines and none of them writes motion.  Its TAA
([[lisp:luvcraft::encode-luvcraft-temporal-resolve]]) avoids touching them in
three ways.

The jitter is folded into the camera.  Every block-world vertex stage
projects clip x as /(relative · right) × x-scale/ over /relative · forward/,
so adding a share of =forward= to =right= and =up= displaces every projected
point by exactly the jitter ([[lisp:luvcraft::shear-luvcraft-camera-lanes]]).
The sky inverts that basis approximately, which only matters at second order
for a smooth gradient.

Motion comes from depth.  The neighbourhood pass unprojects its nearest tap
through this frame's unjittered camera and projects it through the last one;
the sky, which writes no depth, reprojects as a direction.  This covers the
camera, which is almost all motion in a block world.  Critters, particles,
and bodies are left to the YCoCg clamp.

First-person geometry is screen-locked, and its SDF arms write no depth, so
the scene pass is split: the world is drawn, its colour is copied, then hands,
held items, and the crosshair are drawn.  Wherever the finished scene differs
from that copy the texel sorts nearest and its motion is zero.

The resolve writes the colour image presentation already read, at the
extent the frame pacer would draw at full allowance (#MAC668 would move that
pacer onto #IKWHEW's controller).  When the pacer sheds
resolution, the resolve upscales; the post, lens, and readback paths do not
know the difference.  =*luvcraft-temporal-p*= takes effect at the next
reframing.

* Dynamic resolution holds the frame rate from GPU timestamps
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: IKWHEW
:END:

Luft draws its scene at a share of =*render-scale*= chosen by Moppe's
controller ([[lisp:luv:observe-dynamic-resolution]]).  Every few samples it
compares the mean GPU time with 90% of the frame period -- the requested rate
or the display's, whichever is slower -- and moves the share in steps of one
in thirty-two between a half and the whole: sharply down when over, gently up
under a quiet band, and not at all inside it, so a steady view settles on a
step instead of hunting.  The samples taken while a new size reaches the GPU
are passed over.

A scene-only resize keeps the output-sized images.  The new target generation
borrows the resolved, history, composite, and lens images from the one it
replaces and takes custody of them at publication, so the shader resolve's
history survives every step; only MetalFX, whose scaler fixes its input size,
starts over.

The times are the GPU's own.  Metal 4 commits carry a feedback handler,
attached by a small native helper because Lisp cannot make blocks, that
writes each submission's =GPUStartTime= and =GPUEndTime= into a ring Lisp
polls ([[lisp:luv:queue-gpu-time-reader]]).  The CPU stopwatch it replaces
had become a measure of latency: with drawables fetched late from a ring of
frame slots, a frame waits behind those ahead of it, and it read about 14.5 ms
at any scene size.  Apple GPUs still lower their clock until light work
nearly fills the frame, so 12 to 14 ms at 60 Hz is normal and the controller
correctly holds the whole scene there; it sheds only when the clock is
exhausted.  Live on an M-series Mac, a 2.4× scene measured 26.7 ms, stepped
down to a half, held history throughout, and grew back step by step to the
whole when the load was removed.
