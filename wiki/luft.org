#+title: LUFT: packed sites, chains, and the mesh atelier
#+startup: overview

LUFT is a small complement to luv: a way of naming the vertices, edges, faces,
and cells of a cubical world with one fixnum each, and of treating a block
world as algebra rather than as voxels with decorations.  Its first client
is a greenfield renderer, an atelier free of the luvcraft mesher, that draws
a textureless block world with task and mesh shaders reading those fixnums
straight from a GPU buffer.  This page records the vocabulary, the algebra
that makes the surface fall out, the shader-language extensions the GPU path
needed, and the shape of the pipeline.

* A site is a lattice anchor and an extent mask
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: KE4P5F
:END:

A /site/ is a 60-bit unsigned value: four extent bits =XYZT= in the low
positions, then a 24-bit X anchor, a 24-bit Y anchor, and an 8-bit Z anchor.
Each spatial extent bit says the site spans the unit interval forward from its
anchor along that axis, so no extent is a vertex, one is an edge, two a face,
three a cell.  Incidence is therefore canonical: the high X boundary of the
cell at =(x,y,z)= /is/ the YZ face anchored at =(x+1,y,z)=, the same value the
cell at =(x+1,y,z)= calls its low X boundary.  X and Y wrap with the period a
[[lisp:luft:world-domain]] chooses; Z is 256 non-wrapping planes.

The oriented boundary of a site follows from the ordered axes in its extent:
the high boundary along the =i=-th present axis has sign =(-1)^i=, the low
boundary the opposite sign, and =∂∂ = 0= is an executable test in
=luft/tests.lisp=.  Time is a fourth extent bit with no coordinate: "now" is
an ambient origin, so temporal boundaries return an offset rather than a
packed time.  See [[lisp:luft:make-site]] and [[lisp:luft:map-site-boundary]].

* A block world is a 3-chain and its surface is a boundary
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: 9HLYEE
:END:

A [[lisp:luft:chain]] is a finite formal sum of sites with integer
coefficients.  A solid world is the 3-chain with coefficient one on every
solid cell, and its visible surface is not a meshing problem but an algebraic
fact: [[lisp:luft:boundary-chain]] applies #KE4P5F's oriented boundary to
every term.  Two solid neighbours contribute their shared face with opposite
signs, so it cancels; only exposed faces survive, each with coefficient
=+1= when the solid cell lies on its low side along the missing axis and
=-1= when it lies on the high side.  That sign times the face's canonical
orientation is its outward normal, and =∂(∂ solid) = 0= says the surface is
closed.  Because =∂= is linear, editing a cell later means adding or
subtracting one cell's six-term boundary, not remeshing anything.

The luvcraft mesher walks chunk fields on the CPU and emits atlas-textured
quads (see [[file:block-world.org][the block world]]); LUFT's
[[lisp:luft:surface-chain]] is a hash table of signed face sites.  Both are
right for their worlds; the second is what a mesh shader wants.

* The GPU reads the surface as packed 64-bit terms
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: AGVXGM
:END:

A [[lisp:luft:pack-term]] is one 64-bit word: the face site in its own 60
bits and the sign of its unit coefficient in bit 60.  Sixty-one bits keep the
word an immediate fixnum on 64-bit SBCL, and a specialized
=(unsigned-byte 64)= vector of terms is exactly the buffer the shader reads;
no vertex is ever built on the CPU.  The renderer orders the terms by
eight-cell chunk, pads them to whole /bricks/ of 64, and measures one
bounding sphere per brick as four floats — two arrays and one uniform block
are the whole upload.

The three free bits above the sign are deliberately unearned.  A per-face
material would fit there, but a term is a chain term, and materials belong to
cells; the atelier is textureless on purpose until that question is asked
properly.

* Storage buffers and bit fields enter the shader language
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: HFX2LI
:END:

The mathematical shader language of #S4H8DR had textures, samplers, and a
vec4-only uniform block, and no way to read an array of 64-bit words.  Two
additions carry a term into a shader:

- A =:storage-buffer= resource with an =:element= type, indexed by
  =buffer-element=; the array has no declared length, and a bare reference to
  the buffer is an error.  Metal lowers it to =const device T* name
  [[buffer(n)]]= bound through the same argument-table address slot as a
  uniform buffer, and SPIR-V to a =StorageBuffer= block holding one
  =RuntimeArray= with an explicit stride, asking for
  =SPV_KHR_storage_buffer_storage_class= in 1.0 modules.
- =ldb= with a literal =(byte size position)= on a =:uint= or =:uint64=
  value, so a shader unpacks a site with the very words =luft.lisp= uses.
  Metal lowers it to a shift and a mask in the operand's width; SPIR-V to
  two logical shifts, because Vulkan restricts bit-field instructions to
  32-bit operands and the language has no 64-bit constants.

Both are raw representations until interpreted: they carry no quantity, so an
unannotated graph stays valid legacy source and an annotated one still
refuses to mix.  The Metal HAL gained the matching =:storage= buffer usage,
=:storage-buffer= binding type, typed =write-buffer= for unsigned arrays,
and depth-stencil state on mesh pipelines.

* Task workgroups cull bricks; mesh lanes unpack faces
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: VAABY9
:END:

[[file:images/luft-atelier.png]]

The pipeline in =luft/render/shaders.lisp= has three stages and no vertex
buffer.  A task workgroup owns one brick: it reads the brick's sphere,
tests it against the view pyramid with a workgroup-uniform expression, and
emits one mesh workgroup or none, handing the brick index through a
one-word payload.  Each mesh lane then loads one term with =buffer-element=,
extracts anchor, extent, and sign with =ldb=, and writes four vertices and
two triangles.  The extent names the face's two spanning axes in canonical
X<Y<Z order, their cross product is the canonical orientation, and the
sign times that orientation is the outward normal — the boundary operator's
sign convention rendered directly.  A lane whose term is zero, or whose face
turns away from the camera by the exact plane test, scales its quad to a
point instead of branching on output counts, which the language requires to
be uniform.

The fragment stage is textureless by design: a top, side, and bottom tone,
a soft line where faces meet, sun and ambient light, and fog toward the sky.
The whole atelier is =luft/render=: a scene holds the solid chain and its
products, a renderer holds the GPU objects, [[lisp:luft.render:render-to-png]]
draws headlessly, and [[lisp:luft.render:start-viewer]] opens a window with a
fly camera.  Its test turns the camera straight up and expects only sky,
which is the frustum test doing real work.

* Fillets and bevels belong to edge and vertex sites
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: 6TEFOS
:END:

Rounding a crease is not something either face beside it can decide alone,
and in LUFT it does not have to be: the crease is an edge site, its four
surrounding cells are its star, and a vertex site has eight.  The star's
solidity classifies the crease — flat, convex, concave, or saddle — and one
rule places the shared point: move toward the star's /minority/ (the lone
solid cell of a convex crease, the lone air cell of a concave one) so that
the point lands on the sphere or cylinder of radius $r$ centred at
$\text{site} + r\,q$, where $q$ is the minority direction clamped to unit
components.  For edges that is the fillet cylinder point at $r(1-1/\sqrt2)$,
for corners the sphere point at $r(1-1/\sqrt3)$, zero on flat and saddle
stars, and a vertex in the middle of a crease run agrees with its edge, so
creases stay straight.

Because every face incident to a site computes that point from the same star,
subdivided faces meet exactly and the surface is watertight without stitching
or corner patches.  =luft/render/shaders.lisp= subdivides each face 6×6:
boundary points use the site rule, ring points inside the radius project onto
the nearest crease's cylinder or the corner's sphere (a pure one-cell corner
is the exact rounded box), and mixed corners such as a block standing on a
floor blend toward the vertex sphere by distance.  The only CPU help is
[[lisp:luft:chain-cell-bits]], the solid chain as one bit per cell, from
which each mesh lane gathers its sixteen star cells; the rounding radius is a
live frame lane.  The exact morphological answer for the mixed stars would be
a table over the 256 vertex configurations, which #WQ611A's wave-level work
does not need and which this heuristic postpones.

* Woodworking: flat 45-degree chamfers, sanded in the fragment stage
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: RUAWR5
:END:

The =:chamfer= style is the one-ring case of #6TEFOS with a different rule:
a shared point moves by exactly $\tfrac{w}{2}\,q$, half the chamfer width
along its star's clamped minority.  For an edge that is the midline of the
45-degree facet between the two inset faces; for a pure corner it is the one
point where the three chamfer planes meet, and with each corner square split
along the diagonal toward its vertex the corner falls into two flat pieces —
a genuinely chamfered box, or a mitred cove at an inside corner, with no
rounding anywhere.  Shading then comes from the geometry rather than from
interpolated normals: =chamfer-fragment-shader= reads each facet's normal
from the screen-space derivatives of the world position and only sands the
arris between a face and its chamfer, blending toward the face normal within
=*sanding-width*= of the chamfer line.

Mixed stars are approximate here as in the rounded style, and more visibly
so because facets are flat: where a block's chamfered vertical edge meets the
floor's coves the true polyhedral surface is a small extra cove facet
tangent to the floor, which lifts the shared vertex straight up by about
$0.29\,w$, whereas the minority rule moves it diagonally.  The exact answer
is a plane-intersection construction per vertex configuration, the same
256-entry table #6TEFOS postpones.

the recomputed one.

** IDEA Compact surviving faces inside a brick
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: WQ611A
:END:

/Intent./ Today a culled or back-facing lane still costs four vertices and
two degenerate triangles because output counts must be uniform and the
language has no workgroup memory, atomics, or subgroup ballots.  A ballot
and prefix sum in the task stage would let a brick emit only its visible
faces, and a mesh workgroup could serve several sparse bricks.

/Evidence./ The current world draws thousands of faces at trivial cost, so
this is not yet a bottleneck; it is the natural next language extension
after #HFX2LI, and the first that touches wave-level semantics on both
Metal and SPIR-V.

/Done when./ A dense brick facing away from the camera emits zero mesh work,
measured on Metal, without changing the surface encoding of #AGVXGM.
