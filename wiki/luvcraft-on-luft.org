#+title: Luft as a terrain engine, and Luvcraft on it
#+startup: overview

Luft is becoming a terrain engine: the layer that owns cubical worlds --
their coordinates, cells, edits, meshes, light, and streaming -- in the
way a physics engine owns bodies and contacts.  Games sit on it.  Luvcraft
is the largest of them and the one a child plays, so most of this page is
the map of moving it across; the Luft atelier and the isometric wizard are
the others, and the smaller ones keep the engine honest.  The shared
developer tools stay below every game in luv itself (#QRP6YG).

* Luft is a terrain engine, not a game engine
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: IU374J
:END:

Box3D (#K9T2BF) is the model for the kind of thing Luft should be.  It owns
a world and the ids in it, steps that world, answers queries against it,
and reports what changed as buffered facts the caller polls after the step
(#F8H3PW).  It draws nothing and knows nothing about the game; a game
supplies its own bodies' meaning, wires the engine to its renderer, and
keeps its rules to itself.  That boundary is why one physics engine serves
many games.

Luft takes the same position for terrain.  Its fundamental types are the
coordinates and packed sites of #KE4P5F, the cells and the columns of
fibers that hold them, and surfaces as boundaries of 3-chains (#9HLYEE).
On those it offers what is hard and useful about terrain: an edit log,
meshes derived from cells, a colored light fixpoint, residency and
streaming over a large world, and queries -- a ray, a pick, an overlap, a
body moved through the cells.  A game asks; the engine answers and tells it
what changed.

 Luvcraft        isometric wizard      Luft atelier         games

      +-----------------+-----------------+
      |                 |                 |

The layers are a direction of dependency, not a ceremony.  Where a line
turns out to be in an awkward place, it moves.

* What each layer owns
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: W9QMPT
:END:

| Layer | Owns | Does not own |
|-
| HAL and luv | devices, canvas, workbench, lobby, capture, agents' plumbing | any world |
| Luft terrain | sites, cells, fibers, edits, meshing, voxel light, world-source protocol, residency, queries, change facts | GPU resources, characters, palettes |
| Luft renderer | star shading, materials on the GPU, sky and clock, projections, finishing, world text, drawing stages a game may add | input, rules, which materials exist |
| Physics | bodies, contacts, the solver | terrain, which it reaches through queries |
| A game | its palette, world sources, characters, cameras, behaviour, UI | the layers below |

The terrain layer runs without a GPU.  The =luft= system is topology,
meshing, star geometry, and the light solver; =luft/terrain= adds scenes,
materials, realized light, world sources, and streaming, and loads with
neither the renderer nor McCLIM (#TNOILN).

The renderer is a sibling of the terrain rather than a generic renderer
underneath it.  Star shading reads packed sites directly (#AGVXGM), and
that tie is the point: it is why plain cubes cost less here than in
Luvcraft (#8TUEPF).  A game uses the renderer it is given and extends it
with stages, not by replacing it.

* Materials are a protocol a game fills in
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: HYBFRT
:END:

Luvcraft's materials have something worth keeping.  Its atlas is not
painted art but arithmetic: each tile paints a relief height field, the
colour reads that field, and a tangent-space normal map and a roughness
come from the same field (#CHUKWD, #2T8CCH).  That relief is what gives
its grass and stone their almost physically based response.  The paintings
on top of it are mostly not beautiful, and they need not survive.  Luft's
materials are tones by face orientation plus optical facts for light, with
bevels supplying the detail.

A Luft material joins the two, and adds what only Luft can know:

1. *Tones* -- top, side, and bottom albedo, as now.
2. *Relief in world space* -- a height field over the surface rather than a
   tile repeated on each face, so cracks and tufts run across cell
   boundaries along a manifold sheet and the grid disappears without
   needing a bevel.
3. *Response to the topology* -- stars know exactly where an edge is convex
   and where a corner is concave, so a material can wear at edges, gather
   moss or dirt in hollows, and let grass overhang the top of a side face.
4. *Optical facts* -- light opacity, emission, and surface glow, as now.

The engine defines this protocol and the renderer realizes it; the
material set itself belongs to a game.  Luvcraft's 72 kinds become one
palette: their relief painters become relief fields and their colours
become tones, so the grass crosses nearly unchanged and weaker kinds can
be repainted over time.  Whether relief is baked into a texture or
evaluated in the star fragment stage is a question of measured cost
(#Q3KI72).

* Where the line runs today
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: TNOILN
:END:

Five systems now stack the way #W9QMPT draws them.  =luft= is the
topological core.  =luft/terrain=, in =luft/terrain/= and the
=luft.terrain= package, holds the scene and its material field, realized
light, the world-source protocol and the alpine relief, residency, and the
streaming owner/worker handoff up to an accepted replacement.
=luft/renderer= sits on it with the GPU machinery and publishes accepted
replacements into mesh slots.  =luft/simulation= depends on the terrain
alone.  =luft/world=, in the =luft.world= package, is the atelier's world
as terrain content: its palette, the landscapes and alpine large world, and
the fixture scenes.  The atelier, =luft/render=, uses all of them;
=luft.render= uses =luft.terrain= and re-exports the terrain names it always
exported, and the atelier attaches =luft.world= when it loads, so the
renderer's own package never names the world.

A torch is now one game's [[lisp:luft.terrain:surface-attachment]]: a
face-anchored thing made of a material kind.  The terrain keeps an
attachment's support cell solid and its clearance cell clear, refusing
edits that would break either, and an attachment's light comes from its
material; the Luft world's =scene-builder-torch= attaches its flame
material, and the renderer's drawn flame keeps its own tone.  Mesh
decoration now realizes each owner's attachments into frames at their face
chart point, and a material with an /attachment form/ becomes one when a
world source materializes it: a Luvcraft lantern block stands on the solid
cell below it, or hangs from the one above, as a drawn iron-and-glass
lantern whose cell is its clearance.  Lanterns seed the streamed voxel field
at that realized clearance cell; the unoccluded eight-nearest point lights
are removed and the scene uniform is back to 136 floats.  Stars, fixture
bodies, and attachment bodies read the accepted field.  Placing a supported
lantern through either game's edit path realizes its attachment before the
next remesh, retaining the original placement in the source's edit overlay
for reloads.  Unsupported lanterns remain cubes, as on initial materialization.

A game's palette reaches the terrain in two places.  A scene builder takes
a material vocabulary and the placements a cell gets by default, and a
[[lisp:luft.terrain:world-source]] carries the vocabulary its chunks are
painted in; the game answers
[[lisp:luft.terrain:generate-world-source-chunk]] with the cells a chunk
generates, and the terrain materializes, replays edits, and assembles fibers.
=luft/terrain/test= is a second game in miniature -- two materials, a flat
world, and its own mesh preparer -- that runs with neither the renderer nor
the Luft world loaded.

=luvcraft/luft= is the first game outside the atelier.  It answers that
protocol with Luvcraft's own generator and its own palette, and a source
names its spawn through [[lisp:luft.terrain:world-source-spawn]], so the
studio no longer assumes the atelier's large world when it places a
character.  =luvcraft/luft/game= hosts it in a viewer frame of its own
(#J3WZZS), the first game to do so, and what that forced is listed there.

A streaming scene's consumer may realize each finished mesh on the worker
through [[lisp:luft.terrain:prepare-streamed-mesh]], with
[[lisp:luft.terrain:streamed-mesh-preparation-signature]] keeping the mesh
cache honest across live redefinition.  The renderer's
=+render-population-preparer+= builds its CPU population there; without a
preparer a streamed product is simply the mesh.

A body is a terrain query.  =luft/terrain= owns the
[[lisp:luft.terrain:physical-body]] box and asks of the solid where it
collides, where it can stand
([[lisp:luft.terrain:body-support-height]]), and which cells it can walk
between ([[lisp:luft.terrain:search-walking-path]]); a game's character
composes a body with its intentions and turns a path into its own route.

The scene no longer says whether it has a player.  A game decides: the
atelier's =start-viewer= takes =:player-p=, the wizard always walks, and a
study capture asks for the frame to itself.  The walking character, its
controllers, and the world clock sit in =luft/simulation=, generic enough
that the wizard uses them as they are (#CERUZY).

Figures are now a stage a game adds, with per-instance storage rather
than one pose in the scene uniforms. The wizard and Luvcraft's turtles
use it (#CS2DOF), as do Luvcraft's embodied companions (#LEQ5SP); hands
and held devices still need stages.

What still sits on the wrong side of the line:

- The atelier's follow camera, first-person eyes, and orbit stay in its
  studio; the wizard wrote its own follow in a dozen lines over the
  renderer's camera, which is where a game's camera behaviour belongs.
- The atelier shares =luft.render= with the renderer, which is why it
  attaches =luft.world= at load rather than in a package definition.

* What the wizard forced into the engine
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: CERUZY
:END:

The isometric wizard (#BJRZW2) first reached past the protocol in four
places, and each became an engine function rather than an export of the
atelier's internals.

1. *A ray is a terrain query.*  The lattice ray walk lived in the studio and
   returned an atelier =site-inspection=.  It is now
   [[lisp:luft.terrain:raycast-face]], returning the face, the cell, the
   point, and the distance, with [[lisp:luft.terrain:raycast-standing-cell]]
   for a walking game's click: only the top of a solid cell promises
   somewhere to stand.  The atelier's =raycast-site= wraps it in its
   inspection.
2. *Cameras are the renderer's.*  The fly camera, its eased yaw, its basis,
   the two projections, temporal jitter, and the scene uniform packing moved
   from the studio to the renderer's =camera.lisp=, with
   [[lisp:luft.render:camera-pointer-ray]] for the ray through a point on
   the screen under either projection.
3. *A frame is one call.*  Turning a camera into a frame -- jitter, history
   continuity, exposure, uniforms, then every pass -- was the studio's
   =encode-viewer-frame=.  It is now
   [[lisp:luft.render:encode-renderer-view]], which the atelier and the
   wizard both call with their own camera, projection, and figure list.
4. *The figure is instances, not a character.* The uniform packing once read a
   =walking-character= directly. A game now submits a list of
   [[lisp:luft.render:figure-lanes]] -- feet now and a frame ago, gait, and
   headings -- or [[lisp:luft.render:box-figure]] records to an optional
   [[lisp:luft.render:make-figure-drawing]] stage. The renderer does not know
   what a character is; a frame without figures passes NIL. Scene uniforms
   retain a focus point for haze and tilt shift, not a drawing pose.

The scene's =player-p= was the fifth: the renderer drew the figure only when
the terrain said so.  The terrain no longer says (#S5POD8).

* Luvcraft is closer than its code suggests
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: BBKLUG
:END:

Luvcraft is about 54,000 lines: a world model, a CPU mesher, a frontier
light solver, streaming, a 3,200-line renderer, physics, critters, agents,
and a long tail of devices in the world -- terminals, a phone, portals,
video screens, tapes, fireworks.  Luft is about 28,000: the terrain core, a
star-atlas renderer whose plain cubes cost less than Luvcraft's and whose
bevels about one and a half to two times as much (#8TUEPF, #72Y245,
#A4ZWDZ), streaming, a walking character, editing, and a McCLIM atelier on
the shared workbench.  Luft has almost none of the game; Luvcraft has an
older version of nearly all of the engine.

The surprise is the world itself.  Luvcraft's 72 block kinds are every one
an opaque full cube -- =blocks.lisp= says so: "the world has no transparent
cells" -- and a Luft cell is exactly one occupancy bit and one material
from a vocabulary of up to 255.  Every Luvcraft world is already a Luft
world; =scripts/terrain-comparison.lisp= converts one cell for cell
(#PWX5RT).  What differs is what a material means and what lives beside
the cells.

* What maps directly, and what does not
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: R15JJS
:END:

| Concern | Luvcraft | Luft | Crossing |
|-
| Cell | 72 opaque cube kinds | solid bit + material, ≤255 | direct |
| Axes, chunk | Y up; 16×64×16 | Z up; 64×64×255 columns | coordinate map only |
| Material appearance | relief, normal, and colour tiles per face | tones and bevels | the material protocol (#HYBFRT) |
| Block behaviour | tape, film, fountain, lava spring, terminal kinds | none | a game's behaviour table |
| Light | sky and block light, incremental, streamed | RGB4 field, off in the streamed world | stream Luft's field |
| Generator | meadow and alpine relief, caves, crystals, trees, rocks | alpine relief already ported | world sources, pure functions |
| Edits and saves | seed + sparse edit overlay + resume + inhabitants | seed + sparse edits + pose + hour | same shape; extend |

Luvcraft's generator is the easiest substantial thing to move: its noise
and height functions are pure and only its materialization writes chunks,
and the alpine relief already crossed that way.  Saves have the same form
on both sides, a seed and a sparse overlay, so an old Luvcraft save can be
replayed onto a Luft world raised from the ported generator once the
coordinate map turns Y up into Z up and fits 64 cells of height into 255.

About a quarter of Luvcraft, some 13,000 lines of renderer, meshing,
streaming, walking, world text, and terminal walls, is engine that
Luft has already rebuilt and that Luvcraft simply deletes. The sky instead
shares Luvcraft's stronger atmosphere and cloud shadows with Luft's moving
night, moon and aerial perspective (#S84HR6). Roughly
12--15k lines are neutral game code that moves nearly as it is.  The rest
sits on five seams.

* Five seams carry the cost
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: NFNM1C
:END:

1. *Appearance.*  The material protocol of #HYBFRT, realized in the star
   shading at a cost the plain cubes can afford.  The inventory icons, the
   gazetteer's views, and the way a child recognizes a block follow from
   it.

2. *Light.*  Luvcraft's sky and block light is incremental, streamed, and
   paid for by a compiled frontier language; Luft's RGB4 field is solid but
   not streamed.  Streamed light belongs to the terrain engine, and #LX8GZ2
   already names it as the trigger for moving the frontier language below
   both.

3. *Drawing stages.*  About twenty Luvcraft files hang off one session
   protocol whose stages -- scene, world panel, viewmodel, held display,
   HUD -- are Luvcraft's own passes.  Phones, portals, video screens, held
   items, and the hands need stages that a game can add to Luft's frame.
   The renderer grows them one device at a time rather than adopting
   Luvcraft's protocol (#M9MI6Y).  This is the largest piece of work that
   is neither deletion nor a straight port.

4. *Block behaviour.*  Tapes, films, fountains, lava springs, and terminal
   blocks are kinds with behaviour; a Luft cell has only a material.  A
   game keeps a behaviour table keyed by material, with its own per-cell
   state, and learns of edits through the engine's change facts.

5. *Agents' words.*  The gnome's tools and prompts speak integer cells,
   "y is up", and block boxes in the old game. The Luft game now speaks
   integer X/Y/Z cells with Z up, resident terrain rays, routes, source edits,
   and the game's finite inventory (#LEQ5SP). The shared harness still owns
   turns, tools, transcript, and provider lifetime; no terrain query goes
   through an old Luvcraft session.

* The plan
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: Q758FV
:END:

First the engine line is drawn and a small second game proves it; then
Luvcraft becomes a game on the engine one feature at a time.  Each step
leaves every game working.  The old Luvcraft program stays the oracle, and
the game a child starts, until its Luft form replaces it; each feature that
crosses is deleted from the old program rather than kept in parallel
(#WO50UR).

** DONE Separate the terrain engine from the renderer and the atelier
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: S5POD8
:END:

/Intent./ Move world sources, residency, streaming, edits, and the
collision, standing, and route queries out of =luft/renderer= and
=luft/simulation= into a terrain layer that loads without a GPU.  Replace
the streaming scene's knowledge of torches with change facts a game reads.
Move the material set, torches, character, follow camera, and sanctuary
fixtures into the atelier, which becomes one game among others.

/Done when./ The terrain layer loads and passes its tests without the
renderer, names no torch, player, or palette, and the atelier plays as
before.

/Evidence./ The first cut is in place: =luft/terrain= loads alone, with
no renderer, no McCLIM, and no undefined callee, and the renderer suite
passes unchanged on top of it.  The cut split realized light out of the
sky's =lighting.lisp=, moved publication into the renderer's residency,
made the scene's attachment-frame snapshot independent of the torch row
layout, and let =luft/simulation= stand on the terrain.  The second cut
gave the terrain its own directory and package, and replaced its knowledge
of the renderer's population with a preparation hook; a terrain-only image
streams a region through a real worker and reuses its cached star
products.  The third cut
moved the palette, the landscapes, and the fixtures into =luft/world= and
gave the terrain a world-source protocol; streamed alpine chunks, the
mountain sanctuary, and the compiled palette are identical cell for cell to
the code before it, and =luft/terrain/test= plays a second game on the
terrain alone.  #TNOILN lists what remains; the fourth cut made
torches one kind of surface attachment, so the terrain no longer names
them.  The fifth moved collision, standing, and path search into the
terrain, where =luft/terrain/test= routes a body around a wall it cannot
climb.  The sixth removed the scene's =player-p=: the scene, its builder,
streaming snapshots, and world-source scenes no longer carry it, and the
atelier's =start-viewer= takes the choice instead, which the atelier and
=luvcraft/luft= keep as before and study captures turn off.  The isometric
wizard (#BJRZW2) then played over the engine alone, and what it needed moved
below the games (#CERUZY).

** DONE Make the isometric wizard a game of its own

/Intent./ Lift the isometric click-to-walk mode into a small package over
the engine layers alone, bringing its own palette, character, camera, and

/Done when./ The wizard plays from its own system, and the engine changes

/Evidence./ =luft/wizard=, in the =luft.wizard= package, depends on
=luft/renderer= and =luft/simulation= and nothing else: no atelier, no Luft

standing stones on a levelled green, a pond, scattered trees -- with its spawn
on the path, a walking character drawn as the renderer's robed figure, an
isometric camera that follows him and turns by eighths on Q and E, and
click-to-walk.  It opens its own canvas, renderer, and mesh worker, streams
the isle around him, and screenshots through luv's shared offscreen capture;
=./sly play wizard= opens it.  =luft/wizard/test= raises part of the isle
without a GPU, projects a cell up the path onto the screen by hand, casts the
renderer's screen ray back through that point, finds the same cell with the
terrain's ray, and walks him there through the simulation; it also checks
that a standing stone's side and the sky name nowhere to stand.  What the
wizard had to reach for became engine protocol (#CERUZY).  The atelier keeps
its own isometric mode, which now shares that protocol rather than code with
the wizard.

*** DONE Give the wizard an ember journey
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: 7D3HOK
:END:

The first small answer to #DLH49J is /The Ember Walk/: leave the path for the
standing-stone green, kindle its plinth with Space, carry that spell to the
ridge, then awaken the pond-bank plinth. A gold compass waymark stays on the
screen edge when its destination is offscreen; the objective names the next
place, its distance, and when the wizard is close enough to cast. Three
lights finish the journey. R returns to spawn without erasing the lights.

The pointer has a green cell outline and crosshair on a standing surface,
red where a ray finds no floor. Clicks use the terrain's existing bounded
eight-connected A*; gold breadcrumbs show the route rather than promising a
straight walk through a tree. Refused routes say so. Tests walk around a
wall, reject a corner-enclosed destination and an occupied cell, handle an
already-reached cell, and actually traverse all three journey legs.

Kindling adds an emissive amber cell above the stone plinth through the
authored edit protocol and schedules its mesh/light cohort. The world-source
overlay retains it through residency changes. This is a small first spell,
not a claim that the beam reference plate in #YRDFFH is finished: no beam,
fluid volume, or transient-light API was added. The game owns its compact
bitmap HUD and cast sigil; it still depends on neither McCLIM nor the atelier.

=scripts/wizard-journey.lisp= drives a fresh =./sly play wizard= through
screen-space clicks, A*, simulation and each real streamed edit, with no
teleportation. It captures the start, route, and three lights using software
Vulkan and checks that returning to spawn keeps completion. The source cache
can become resident before the collision cohort is published; movement now
waits for the published collision chunk, so cold startup cannot drop the
wizard through not-yet-playable ground. Progress remains session-local:
save/resume, branching journeys and the larger worksheet's fortress loop are
still open.

** NEXT Give materials tones, relief, and edge response
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: Q3KI72
:END:

/Intent./ Realize #HYBFRT in the star shading: world-space relief with a
normal and roughness, and wear and cavity terms from the stars' convex and
concave edges.  Measure the cost of baked and evaluated relief on the
exported Luvcraft world (#PWX5RT) before choosing.

/Done when./ The exported world renders with Luvcraft's palette, every
kind recognizably itself and its grass at least as good, at a measured
cost.

/Evidence./ A [[lisp:luft.terrain:material-kind]] now names a relief field,
its strength, a roughness, and an edge wear, packed into the
descriptor row's fourth word by
[[lisp:luft.terrain:pack-terrain-surface]].  The star vertex stage reads
the edge's convexity from the record's occupied and empty cell masks; the
fragment stage cuts the relief in world space, bends the normal by it,
darkens its hollows, lightens convex edges and darkens concave ones, and
adds a rough sun highlight.  The first eight fields were grass, stone,
grain, wood, leaves, planks, snow, and gloss.  Luvcraft's 72 kinds each
choose one; the atelier's palette chooses too, and its earth is still one
kind for grass tops and dirt sides, so it is grass all over.  Evaluated
relief costs nothing measurable: a 1920 by 1080 frame of the alpine world
on chapel's Vulkan takes 4.1 to 4.3 ms of GPU with every relief on or every
relief off, at the queue's whole-millisecond resolution, so nothing is
baked.  Grass and sand now have grain, bark ridges, leaves clump, and
terrace edges catch the light.  Wool and tapestry have a ninth field,
cloth: rounded threads crossing over and under.  Luvcraft's stone family is
moved toward a warm grey of the same brightness by

=terminal-focus-camera-pose= solver through a geometric surface adapter.

free-camera pose; riding uses the same return rather than its own flag.

=scripts/capture-terminal-camera.lisp= dispatches portable Tab, typed
=printf=, Return and Shift-Tab events in both native software-Vulkan games,
checks actual PTY output, and captures the same 800 by 600 wall before,
0.1 seconds into entry, focused, 0.1 seconds into return, and returned.

focused text fits between both HUD bands. The fatal canvas watchdog is
disabled for these slow captures. SDL offscreen cannot capture a relative
pointer: the harness supplies the routing flag and disables the old game's

look fighting the return. Luft already leaves modal focus with the pointer

framing equivalence, Shift-Tab dismissal and intermediate return FOV; removing
